# Safety When You're Renting to People You Don't Know

This is the concern people have and rarely say out loud, especially women considering this alone. It deserves a direct answer rather than being skipped because it's uncomfortable.

The honest version: this is a manageable risk, and almost all of the management happens before anyone moves in.

## Screening is the safety plan

Nearly everything that protects you happens at the front end. Once someone has keys, your options narrow dramatically — which is why the pressure to fill a room fast is a safety issue and not just a financial one.

**Run the full process on everyone.** Written application, identity verification, income verification, background and eviction history, and previous landlord contact. Every single applicant, no exceptions for people who seem nice or come recommended.

**Verify identity properly.** Government ID, and confirm the name matches the application and everything you're running checks on. This is basic and frequently skipped.

**Call the previous landlord, not just the current one.** The current one may want them gone.

**Meet in person before you decide.** Not just because it's informative — because it's a filter. People who won't meet before signing are telling you something.

**Do a video or in-person walkthrough with them.** How someone behaves in your home when they're trying to make a good impression is data.

## Trust the conversation

Screening reports catch documented history. They don't catch everything, and the in-person read is doing real work that no report replicates.

Pay attention to: evasiveness about basic questions, a story that shifts, pressure to move fast or skip steps, resistance to any part of your process, an unwillingness to provide references, or anything that makes you feel like you need to justify your own requirements.

**If something feels off, that's an answer.** You do not owe anyone an explanation for declining an application, and "I went with someone else" is a complete sentence.

An empty room costs you one month. That number is small compared to living with a bad decision.

## Set up the house so it's not a question

**Locks on every bedroom door**, including yours. This should be non-negotiable and it's a normal expectation in shared housing, not an insult.

**A separate entrance where possible**, which reduces shared-space contact generally.

**Exterior lighting and visibility** at entrances.

**Change the locks between every resident.** Every time. Copies exist that you don't know about.

**Keep valuables and documents secured** in your own space.

**A doorbell camera at exterior entrances.** Common, normal, and disclosed in your lease.

## Set expectations that protect you

**Guests, in writing.** How often, how long, and whether overnight guests are fine. The main way an unvetted person ends up living in your house is as someone's "guest" who never leaves.

**No unauthorized occupants**, stated plainly in the lease.

**Nobody gets keys except a screened, leased resident.** Not a partner who's "over all the time," not a friend crashing for a month.

That guest clause is doing more safety work than most people realize.

## Practical habits

**Tell someone your showing schedule.** When you're meeting an applicant, a friend knows where and when.

**Show the property with someone else home** when you can, especially early meetings.

**Keep documentation of everything** — applications, screening, the lease, and any concerning incidents. Written records matter if a situation ever escalates.

**Know your local emergency and non-emergency numbers**, and don't hesitate to use them if a situation warrants it.

## If someone feels unsafe after they've moved in

This is the harder scenario and it's worth thinking about before it happens.

**Document everything immediately** — dates, what happened, any messages. Contemporaneous notes matter.

**If you're in danger, that's a police matter**, not a landlord matter. Don't try to handle a genuine safety situation as a lease dispute.

**For a non-emergency but serious situation**, a negotiated early exit is usually faster than a formal process. Offering someone a way out — sometimes even returning a deposit you'd be entitled to hold — is frequently the cheapest and fastest resolution.

**Talk to an attorney early** if it's escalating. Don't wait until it's a crisis to find out what your options are.

## The honest framing

Most house hacking is uneventful. The overwhelming majority of residents are ordinary people who wanted a place to live and pay their rent on time.

But "mostly fine" isn't a plan. The plan is: screen everyone the same way, set the house up properly, write the guest rules down, and treat your own discomfort as legitimate information rather than something to talk yourself out of.

Do those things and you've addressed nearly all of what's actually addressable.
