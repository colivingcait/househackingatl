# Why Cash Flow Isn't the Only Number That Matters

There's a strain of real estate advice that treats cash flow as the only legitimate measure. If it doesn't cash flow, it's not a deal. Everything else is speculation.

It's a useful discipline and a poor complete framework — and following it strictly leads people toward a specific kind of property that often underperforms.

> **Not tax or investment advice.** I'm a real estate agent, not a CPA or financial advisor. General education only; your situation is your own.

## What cash flow actually measures

Rent in, minus everything out. It's real, it's spendable, and you need some.

But notice what it *doesn't* capture: the loan balance dropping every month, the property changing value, and the tax treatment of the income. Three of the four returns a property produces are invisible to a cash flow calculation.

## The properties strict cash-flow investing pushes you toward

When cash flow is the only filter, the math points at the cheapest properties relative to rent. That usually means older housing stock, weaker locations, and higher maintenance.

Those properties can be excellent. They also tend to share characteristics worth naming: more repairs, more turnover, more management intensity, weaker long-term appreciation, and residents in more precarious financial situations, which means more vacancy and more collection issues.

The spreadsheet cash flow and the realized cash flow on those deals often differ significantly — because the spreadsheet assumed a maintenance number and a vacancy number that the property doesn't respect.

Meanwhile, a property in a stronger location with a lower rent-to-price ratio might produce less on paper and more in reality, while also doing better on appreciation and attracting residents who stay.

## The returns you can't see

**Debt paydown** happens every month regardless of the market. Early on it's small, then it accelerates, and over a long hold it frequently exceeds total cash flow. It's the most reliable return in real estate and it appears nowhere in a cash flow calculation.

**Appreciation** works on the entire property value, not on your down payment. On a low-down-payment purchase, a modest percentage gain is enormous relative to what you actually invested. Unreliable year to year, historically significant over decades.

**Tax treatment** can mean that income you did receive is partly sheltered by deductions you didn't pay cash for. That changes the after-tax picture in ways the pre-tax number doesn't show.

Add those to modest cash flow and the total return often beats a high-cash-flow property that's doing nothing else.

## Where the cash-flow discipline is right

I don't want to talk you out of it entirely, because the underlying warning is sound.

**You need enough cash flow to survive.** Vacancies happen, water heaters fail, and a property that requires you to feed it every month is a liability that will eventually force a decision at a bad time.

**Appreciation-only bets are dangerous.** "It doesn't cash flow but the area is growing" has bankrupted a lot of people. Buying something you can't hold through a downturn means you might not be there for the upside.

**Nobody eats equity.** Debt paydown and appreciation are real, and they're locked in the property until you sell or refinance. You still have to pay for groceries.

The right frame isn't "cash flow doesn't matter." It's: **cash flow is the floor, not the score.** Enough to hold comfortably through bad months — then evaluate the whole return.

## What this means for a house hack

House hacking sidesteps a lot of this tension, which is part of why it works so well as a starting point.

Your "cash flow" is a reduced housing cost, which you'd otherwise be paying to a landlord. That's not a marginal spread you're squeezing out of a property — it's a large fixed expense you're eliminating.

Meanwhile the property is doing everything else: debt paydown, potential appreciation, and whatever tax treatment applies. You're capturing the invisible returns while your visible return is simply *not paying rent.*

That's why a house hack that "only" gets you to a substantially reduced housing cost is often a stronger financial outcome than a rental property with better-looking cash flow numbers.

## The practical takeaway

When you compare two properties, run the whole picture: cash flow, principal reduction over your expected hold, a conservative appreciation assumption, and a conversation with your CPA about the tax side.

Then apply the floor test — could you hold this comfortably through several empty months?

If the answer's yes and the total return is strong, the fact that the monthly number is modest isn't the disqualifier a lot of people treat it as.
