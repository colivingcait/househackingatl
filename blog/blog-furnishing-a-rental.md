# How to Furnish a Rental Without Overspending

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

Furnishing is where first-time house hackers most often blow their budget, and it happens the same way every time: they buy the furniture, feel good about it, and then spend the same amount again on the hundred small things nobody thinks about.

Here's how to do it deliberately.

## Two budgets, not one

Split your furnishing money into two buckets from the start.

**The furniture.** Bed, mattress, dresser, seating, a table if the space needs one. This is the part everyone plans for.

**Everything else.** Cookware, dishes, glassware, utensils, towels, sheets, a shower curtain, a trash can, a lamp that actually works, hangers, a bath mat, a can opener.

That second bucket is routinely as large as the first and it never appears in anyone's plan. Budget for it separately or it will eat your reserve.

## Spend where it's touched daily

The rule that saves the most money: **spend on what people use every day, save on everything else.**

**Worth spending on:**
- **The mattress.** Above everything else. It's the single largest driver of whether someone is comfortable, and it's the thing they'll mention if it's bad.
- **A desk chair,** if the space is likely to house someone who works from home.
- **Lighting.** Cheap and transformative. A dim room reads as a bad room.
- **The internet connection.** Not furniture, but the most-used amenity in the house by a wide margin.

**Not worth spending on:**
- Decorative anything
- Expensive seating
- Matching sets
- Anything trend-driven that'll look dated in three years

The instinct to make a space beautiful is understandable and mostly misdirected. People pay for comfortable and functional. They don't pay extra for styled.

## Buy for durability, not for looks

You're furnishing something that will be used by several different people over several years. That's a different job than furnishing your own bedroom.

Favor solid over veneer where it's cheap to do so. Favor darker fabrics and simple shapes. Favor things that can be wiped down. Avoid anything with a fragile finish or a complicated assembly you'd have to redo.

The question isn't "would I like this?" It's "will this still be fine after four residents?"

## Where to buy

**Facebook Marketplace and estate sales** are where the real savings are, particularly for solid wood dressers and tables. Older furniture is frequently better built than new budget furniture at the same price.

**Big-box basics** are fine for the things nobody notices — hangers, shower curtains, trash cans, kitchen basics.

**Buy the mattress new.** This is the one place not to save. New, and reasonably good.

**Watch for turnover in your own network.** People moving, downsizing, or relocating often want furniture gone more than they want money for it.

## Standardize across spaces

If you're furnishing more than one room, buy the same things.

Same bed frame, same mattress, same dresser, same lamp. It costs nothing extra and it pays off constantly: you can replace one item without rethinking a room, you know exactly what to reorder, and pricing rooms fairly gets simpler when they're comparably equipped.

The same logic applies to paint. One wall color across every rentable space makes every turnover faster.

## The thing that actually matters

A furnished space charges a premium because someone can arrive with a suitcase and start living.

Which means **half-furnished is worse than unfurnished.** A room with a nice bed and no cookware, no towels, and one set of sheets charges a premium and doesn't deliver the thing the premium was for. That's how you get bad feedback and short stays.

Before you list, do the test: could someone move in tonight with a suitcase and cook dinner, shower, and sleep? If any part of that fails, you're not done.

## Build a replacement line

Furniture wears out. Mattresses have a lifespan. Things disappear.

Set aside a small amount monthly for furniture replacement, separate from your maintenance reserve. Otherwise you'll hit year three needing two mattresses at once and treat it as an emergency instead of a scheduled expense.

## Take the photos before anyone moves in

The one afternoon your space will ever look perfect is the day you finish furnishing it and before a single person has lived there.

Photograph everything then — daylight, lights on, blinds open, floor clear. Those are your listing photos for the next several turnovers, and you'll never get a better shot at them.

## The honest math

Furnishing costs real money up front, and it's the main reason to consider starting unfurnished on a first house hack. You can always furnish later, once you know how the house operates and you have income covering the spend.

But if the demand near you supports it — people relocating, on contract, in transition — furnished rents meaningfully higher, fills faster, and attracts people who are easy to live with because they're barely home.

Just go in knowing it's two budgets, not one.
