# What to Look For on a Walkthrough That Photos Never Show You

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

Listing photos are marketing. They're shot wide, lit well, and framed to hide whatever the seller would rather you not think about. That's not dishonest — it's just what photos are for.

Which means the walkthrough is where you actually evaluate a house hack. And most people spend that walkthrough looking at the same things they already saw online.

Here's what to look at instead.

## Stand in the doorway of every bedroom and look out

Not into the room. Out of it.

What does a resident see when they open their door in the morning? A hallway? Directly into your kitchen? The back of someone's couch?

This tells you more about how the house will feel than any floor plan. A bedroom that opens onto a shared living space is a bedroom people leave less often, and residents who never come out of their rooms are residents who don't renew.

## Follow the path from every entrance to every bedroom

Walk it. Physically.

If someone comes home at 11pm, what do they pass? Whose door? Whose television? A house where the path from the front door to the back bedroom crosses the living room is a house where one person's schedule affects everyone.

Do the same for bathrooms. The route from bedroom to bathroom at 6am matters, especially if it passes through a common area.

## Look at the doors themselves

Do the bedroom doors have locks, or just handles? Can they be added? Are the doors solid or hollow?

Hollow-core doors transmit sound almost completely. That's a cheap fix, but it's a fix you should know about before you're comparing two offers.

## Open every closet

Storage is one of the least discussed and most consequential features of a shared house. Every resident brings a life's worth of belongings, and if there's nowhere to put it, it ends up in the shared space — where it slowly becomes a source of friction nobody addresses.

Count the closets. Look at the garage, the attic access, the basement storage. A house with generous storage runs quieter than one without.

## Check the kitchen for a second fridge

Not whether one exists — whether one *fits*.

Refrigerator space is a genuinely common source of low-grade tension in a shared house, and it's solvable if the kitchen has room for a second unit with a nearby outlet. Look for the space and the plug. It's the kind of thing you'll never notice in photos and will think about every week if it's missing.

Same for laundry. Is it accessible from common space, or does someone have to walk through a bedroom or a private area to get to it?

## Listen

Stand still in the middle of the house for thirty seconds and just listen.

Road noise. HVAC. Whether you can hear the upstairs bathroom from the living room. Ask the agent to walk upstairs while you stand below.

Sound travel is a top-three determinant of whether people are happy in a shared house, and it is completely invisible in every photo ever taken.

## Look up, and look at the corners

Water damage announces itself in ceilings and corners before anywhere else. Staining, bubbling paint, a patch that's a slightly different color. In basements, look where the wall meets the floor and check whether anything is stored directly on the concrete — or whether everything's on pallets, which tells you what the owner knows.

Smell matters too. A finished lower level that smells faintly of damp has a problem regardless of how it looks.

## Count the parking, out loud

Stand in the driveway and count how many cars fit without anyone blocking anyone in. Then look at the street. Is there parking? Is it permitted? Is it already full at 2pm on a Tuesday?

If you're renting to multiple adults, this is a daily-friction issue that never appears in a listing.

## Check the electrical panel

You don't need to be an expert. Look at the panel, take a photo of it, note whether it looks original to the house and whether there's obvious space for additional circuits.

If your plan involves a second kitchen, a washer/dryer, or window units, this becomes relevant fast — and it's much better to have the photo when you're getting a quote than to guess.

## The question to hold the whole time

Underneath all of this is one question: *can two people live here without constantly being in each other's way?*

The walkthrough is where you find out. Photos will tell you a house is beautiful. Standing in it tells you whether it works.

## Bring a phone and a plan

Take photos of everything boring — the panel, the water heater, the crawlspace, the parking. Nobody photographs the boring parts, and those are the parts you'll want to look at again when you're deciding.

And go twice if you're serious. Once during the day, once in the evening. Houses are different at 7pm than they are at noon, and so are the streets they're on.
