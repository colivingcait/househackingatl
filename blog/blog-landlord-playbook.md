# The House Hacking Landlord Playbook

Becoming a landlord is the part of house hacking people think least about and worry most about. The financing makes sense, the property makes sense, and then there's a person living in your house paying you money and you're not sure what you're supposed to do.

This is the whole job in one place. Nine things, roughly in the order you'll need them.

## 1. Screen the same way, every time

The single most consequential habit. Same application, same verification, same questions, for every applicant — including the ones you like immediately and especially the ones who come recommended.

Written application. Income verification against a standard you set in advance. Background and rental history. A real conversation. A written lease.

The instinct to be casual because someone seems nice is the most expensive instinct in this business. Consistency isn't distrust — it's what protects you and what makes you fair.

## 2. Use a real lease, even for one room

A handshake is not an agreement, it's a memory. And memories diverge.

Your lease should cover the basics — term, rent, deposit, notice — plus the things specific to shared housing: which spaces are private, which are shared, guest expectations, quiet hours, parking, utilities, and what happens if someone leaves early.

Write it once, reuse it forever. Every unusual situation you encounter becomes a clause you add for next time.

## 3. Have the awkward conversation before move-in

Guests. Noise. The living room. The kitchen. Cleaning. Parking. The thermostat.

Ten minutes of unglamorous questions prevents most of what would otherwise become a problem two months in. Almost every conflict in a shared house traces back to an assumption nobody checked — not a disagreement, an assumption.

## 4. Automate rent collection from day one

Same platform, same due date, every resident, no exceptions. Automated.

The moment you're accepting Venmo whenever someone gets around to it, you've created a job for yourself and a norm you'll have to undo later. Rent should arrive without you thinking about it, and late should be obvious rather than fuzzy.

## 5. Document everything at move-in

Photos of the condition of every space, from angles you'll be able to replicate at move-out. Signed lease. Deposit recorded. Keys logged, and the lock changed since the last resident.

This takes twenty minutes and it's the difference between a clean deposit conversation and an argument with someone who lives in your house.

## 6. Respond fast — it's most of the job

Set an expectation and meet it. Urgent same day, everything else within a few days.

Residents forgive things breaking. They don't forgive being ignored. Responsiveness is the largest driver of whether people renew, and a renewal is worth far more than the cost of being prompt.

The same applies to small things. A house where the broken cabinet gets fixed in a week is a house people take care of. A house where it's been broken since March teaches everyone that nothing gets fixed here.

## 7. Reserve for maintenance before you need it

Set aside a percentage of collected rent monthly and treat it as spent. Then build your vendor list — plumber, electrician, HVAC, handyman, cleaner — *before* something breaks.

Finding a plumber at 9pm on a Sunday means emergency pricing from whoever answers. Having a number in your phone means a text and a normal rate.

## 8. Address problems small and early

The skill you're actually developing isn't real estate. It's saying something small, early, without making it a whole thing.

*"Hey, can we figure out a dish system?"* on day four is nothing. On day sixty it's a confrontation, because now it's about two months of accumulated resentment rather than dishes.

This is harder when you live there — you'll see this person tomorrow — which is exactly why it has to happen early, while it's still small enough to say casually.

## 9. Be rigorous about systems, warm about people

This is the frame that makes all of the above work.

New house hacking landlords struggle with the dual role. You're someone's housemate *and* the person who owns the building. That feels contradictory, so people pick a side — either businesslike to the point of cold, or so friendly they can't enforce anything.

You don't have to pick. Be rigorous about the *systems*: application, lease, due date, documentation. Be warm about the *people*: answer texts, fix things, remember what they told you about their job.

The systems are what let you be warm. When the rules are clear, written, and applied to everyone equally, you're never making a judgment call about a specific person — you're following what you both agreed to. That's what makes friendliness sustainable rather than exhausting.

## What this adds up to

Most of being a good landlord isn't complicated. It's consistency, documentation, and responsiveness — done every time, not when you feel like it.

The people who find this exhausting are usually the ones improvising each decision. The ones who find it easy built the systems once and now just follow them.

Build them in year one and this becomes a few hours a month for as long as you own the property.
