# Basement, Bonus Room, or Spare Bedroom: Which Space Actually Rents?

If you're looking around your house wondering which unused space to rent out, you're usually choosing between three: a spare bedroom, a bonus room, or the basement.

They're not equivalent. One of them earns close to the others for a fraction of the work, one is frequently a disappointment, and one is worth real money if a single feature is present.

## The spare bedroom

**What it earns:** the least of the three, but it's not close to nothing.

**What it costs to prepare:** almost nothing. A lock on the door, maybe a bed and a dresser, a good listing.

**The catch:** you're sharing everything. Kitchen, living room, often a bathroom. The rent is lower because the product is less private — that's the whole trade.

**Worth it when:** you want to start immediately, you don't want to spend money before you see whether you like this, or you're testing the concept before committing to a conversion.

The bedroom's real advantage is speed. You can go from deciding to collecting rent inside a month, and every month you spend not renting is a month of full housing cost. A lot of people spend six months planning a basement conversion when they could have been earning from a bedroom the whole time.

## The bonus room over the garage

**What it earns:** usually the least per square foot of the three, which surprises people because it's often the biggest.

**What it costs to prepare:** varies wildly, and often more than expected.

**The catch:** it's typically the hardest space to separate. Access is frequently through the house. It rarely has its own bathroom and adding one means running plumbing to the far end of the structure. Heating and cooling are often an afterthought — these rooms are famously the coldest and hottest in the house.

**Worth it when:** it already has a bathroom and reasonable access, or when you're renting it as a bedroom-equivalent rather than trying to make it a unit.

The honest read: bonus rooms look like the obvious answer because they're big and unused, and they're the space most likely to disappoint. Price out the bathroom and the HVAC before you get attached.

## The basement or terrace level

**What it earns:** the most, by a significant margin — *if* one condition is met.

**The condition:** a separate exterior entrance.

That single feature is the difference between a room in your house and somewhere someone lives. It changes who's willing to rent it, how long they stay, and what they'll pay. A lower level you can only reach by walking through your living room earns much closer to spare-bedroom rent than to unit rent, no matter how nicely it's finished.

**What it costs to prepare:** anywhere from furniture and a lock to a genuine construction project. Depends entirely on what's already there.

**Worth it when:** the entrance exists or can be added at a sane cost, and there's a bathroom or a straightforward way to add one.

In the Atlanta metro this is the most common house hack for a structural reason — our sloped lots mean walk-out lower levels are everywhere. A lot of housing stock here has the key feature almost by accident.

## The comparison that actually matters

Rank them by **return on effort**, not by rent:

**Spare bedroom** — lowest rent, near-zero cost, immediate. Highest return on effort by a wide margin.

**Basement with an existing entrance and bathroom** — highest rent, modest cost, quick. The best combination available if your house has it.

**Basement needing a conversion** — highest rent, real cost and timeline. Worth it, but not as a first move.

**Bonus room** — middling rent, unpredictable cost. Usually the last one I'd do.

## What to actually do

If you're not sure whether you want to share your home at all, start with the bedroom. It costs nothing to try and it answers the question that matters most, which is whether you like this.

If your basement already has an entrance and a bathroom, start there instead. That's the highest-value space in most houses and you're leaving money on the table every month it sits empty.

If your basement needs work, rent a bedroom first and let that income help fund the conversion. You'll learn how the house operates before you spend real money changing it — and the conversion you design after six months of experience will be better than the one you'd design today.

## The question underneath all three

Whichever space you pick, the same thing determines what it earns: **how separate is it?**

Separate entrance, separate bathroom, real distance from your living space. Every step toward separation moves the rent up and the friction down. Every shared element moves both the other way.

That's not a reason to avoid the shared version — plenty of people rent a bedroom happily for years. It's just the frame for deciding what a space is worth before you spend anything on it.
