# How to Find Good Housemates Without Relying on Luck

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

Ask a house hacker what makes or breaks the whole thing and they'll say the same word: people.

Ask them how they find good ones and most will say some version of *"I got lucky."*

That's not a strategy, and it's also usually not true. The people who consistently end up with good housemates aren't luckier — they just have a process, and they run it every single time.

## The instinct that costs the most

Here's the thing that gets people: the desire to be casual about it.

They seem nice. They're a friend of a friend. They need a place fast and you have a room sitting empty. Running an application feels cold, feels like you're treating a person like a transaction, feels like it might make things weird before they've even moved in.

So you skip it. And most of the time nothing bad happens — which is exactly why the habit sticks until the time it doesn't.

Be warm as a person and rigorous as a process. Those are not in tension. The process is what lets you be warm later, because when the rules are clear and applied to everyone equally, you're never making a judgment call about a specific person. You're just following the thing you both agreed to.

## Run the same process for everyone

Consistency is the whole thing. Same application, same verification, same questions, same standards — for every single applicant, including the ones you like immediately and especially the ones who come recommended.

That means:

**A written application, every time.** No exceptions for friends of friends.

**Income verification.** A common benchmark is income at some multiple of the rent — pick your standard, write it down, and apply it uniformly.

**Background and rental history.** Whatever screening you use, use it for everyone.

**A real conversation before you decide.** Not an interrogation. A conversation. You're choosing who shares your home, and twenty minutes tells you a lot.

**A written lease.** Even for a single room. Even for someone you like.

## The conversation is where the signal is

Screening reports tell you about someone's history. The conversation tells you whether you'll actually enjoy living with them, and that's the part you can't outsource.

Questions worth asking, in a normal human way:

- What does your schedule usually look like?
- Do you cook much?
- What's your ideal living situation — do you like a social house, or do you mostly keep to yourself?
- Have you shared a place before? What worked and what didn't?
- What would make you want to move out?

That last one is underrated. People tell you their dealbreakers if you ask directly, and a mismatch you find in a conversation is much cheaper than one you find in month three.

Also pay attention to how they talk about previous housemates. Someone whose last three living situations were all ruined by other people is telling you something.

## Say the awkward parts out loud before they move in

Almost every conflict in a shared house traces back to an assumption nobody checked.

So check them. Before anyone signs:

- **Guests.** How often, how long, overnight or not.
- **Noise.** What time does the house get quiet?
- **Shared space.** Is the living room communal, or is it primarily yours?
- **Kitchen.** Shared shelves or assigned? Whose food is whose?
- **Cleaning.** Who does what, how often, and what happens when someone doesn't.
- **Parking.** Which spot is whose.

Ten minutes of unglamorous conversation prevents most of what would otherwise become a problem. And someone who reacts badly to being asked these questions has told you something valuable for free.

## Where good housemates actually come from

**Referrals from current residents** are the highest quality source by a wide margin. People refer people they'd be comfortable living with, and they have skin in the game.

**Your own channels.** If you can generate applicants directly rather than depending entirely on a platform, you control the cost and the flow. That's worth building over time.

**Listing platforms** work and they're the default for a reason. Just know that volume isn't quality — more applicants means more screening, not less.

## Say no more often than feels comfortable

The single most common mistake is filling a room fast instead of filling it well.

An empty room costs you one month of rent. The wrong person costs you months of friction, a possible turnover anyway, and the actual quality of your daily life. The math is not close, and it's worth remembering when the room has been empty three weeks and you're getting antsy.

If you're not sure, that's an answer.

## The bar

You're not looking for a friend. You're looking for someone considerate, financially stable, and honest about how they live.

Find that, run the same process every time, and "luck" starts happening a lot more often.
