# How to Handle a Housemate Who Isn't Working Out

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

Most of what goes wrong in a shared house is small and fixable. Occasionally it isn't, and you have someone in your home who's genuinely not working out.

This is the situation people most fear before they start house hacking, and the one they're least prepared for when it happens. Here's how to think about it.

> **One note:** if a situation involves nonpayment, a lease violation, or anything that might end in a formal process, talk to an attorney who handles this in your area. The requirements are specific and getting them wrong is expensive. This post is about the part before that.

## Most of it isn't what you think

Before assuming a situation is unfixable, check which of these it actually is.

**A never-stated expectation.** Guests, noise, the living room, the kitchen. If nobody ever said it out loud, this isn't a difficult person — it's a gap in the setup, and it's usually fixed in one conversation.

**An accumulated small thing.** Something you let slide for two months that's now a pattern. Also fixable, just less comfortable than it would have been on day four.

**A genuine mismatch.** Not a bad person, just wrong for this house. Different schedule, different standard of tidiness, different idea of what shared means. Sometimes solvable, sometimes not.

**An actual problem.** Nonpayment, damage, dishonesty, making other residents uncomfortable. Different category, different response.

The first two are most of what people label "a difficult housemate," and they're the ones you can solve.

## Address it early and small

The whole skill here is saying something small, early, without making it a whole thing.

*"Hey, can we figure out a dish system?"* on day four is nothing. On day sixty it's a confrontation, because by then you're not raising an issue — you're raising two months of accumulated frustration, and they'll hear all of it.

Specific, immediate, low-stakes. Not a summary of everything they've done. One thing, now.

## When you need a real conversation

If small corrections haven't worked, have a direct one.

**In person, at a neutral time.** Not in the moment, not while you're annoyed, not by text.

**Name the specific behavior, not the character.** "The kitchen's been left overnight most days this month" — not "you're inconsiderate."

**Say what you need.** Specifically enough that they'd know whether they'd done it.

**Ask if something's going on.** Sometimes there is, and it changes what the right response is. Sometimes there isn't, and you've still demonstrated you asked.

**Confirm in writing afterward.** Short and friendly — here's what we talked about, here's what we agreed. Not as a threat; as a record you both have.

## When it's a mismatch, say so kindly

If it's genuinely not working and neither of you is the problem, the honest conversation is often a relief for both people.

Something like: *"I don't think this setup is a great fit for either of us. I'd rather talk about a timeline that works than have us both be uncomfortable for another six months."*

That's not a confrontation, and it's frequently met with agreement. People know when a living situation isn't working.

Then handle the timeline according to your lease, in writing, with real notice.

## When it's an actual problem

Nonpayment, damage, dishonesty, or behavior that makes others in the house uncomfortable is a different category.

Document everything from the moment you notice. Communicate in writing. Follow whatever your lease specifies. And talk to a professional about your options in your jurisdiction before you take action — the process is specific and doing it wrong can cost you far more than the situation itself.

Don't try to handle a serious situation informally because you live there and it feels awkward to escalate. That instinct is understandable and it's how small problems become large ones.

## What this teaches you for next time

Every difficult situation points at something in your process.

**Was it a screening miss?** What would have caught it — a reference call, a question you didn't ask, a gut feeling you overrode because the room had been empty three weeks?

**Was it a setup miss?** Something the house structurally invites — no storage, so belongings spread; no assigned parking, so it's a weekly negotiation.

**Was it an expectations miss?** Something never said before move-in that should have been.

Fix that thing. Add the question to your screening, the clause to your lease, the item to your move-in conversation. Your process should get better every time something goes wrong, and after a few years it will have absorbed most of the ways this can go sideways.

## The reassurance

This is rarer than the fear of it suggests. Most residents are fine. Most problems are small. Most of what feels like a difficult person is a conversation that didn't happen when it should have.

And the thing that prevents nearly all of it is the boring stuff: screening the same way every time, saying the awkward things before move-in, and addressing small things while they're still small.

That's not a personality trait. It's a process, and you can just decide to run it.
