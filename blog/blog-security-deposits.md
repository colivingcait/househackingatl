# Security Deposits: How Much, and How to Hold Them

The deposit is the least interesting part of renting a room and one of the most common sources of a bad ending. Almost all of that is preventable with decisions you make before anyone moves in.

> **Not legal advice.** Deposit handling is governed by state and sometimes local law, and the requirements vary. Verify the specifics for where your property is — this is general practice, not a rulebook.

## What a deposit is actually for

It covers damage beyond normal wear and tear, unpaid rent, and unpaid charges the lease provides for.

It is **not** a cleaning fee, not last month's rent, and not a general cushion for you. Treating it as any of those is how disputes start.

**The wear-and-tear line matters.** Carpet showing normal use, minor scuffs, faded paint — that's the cost of owning a rental, not a deduction. A hole in the wall, a broken door, damage from something that shouldn't have happened — that's different.

## How much to collect

Most places set a maximum, and many operators simply collect a standard amount tied to the rent.

Things worth weighing:

**Higher deposits reduce your applicant pool.** Especially for room rentals, where people are often moving with limited cash. A deposit that's too large will slow your fill time.

**Lower deposits leave you exposed** on furnished spaces, where there's more of your property in the room.

**Furnished spaces reasonably carry more.** You're putting real assets in there.

**Be consistent.** Same standard for everyone. Varying deposits by applicant, based on a feeling, is the kind of inconsistency you don't want to have to explain.

Check what your state permits before setting your number, and write the amount into the lease.

## Where the money goes

This is the part people handle casually and shouldn't.

**Don't spend it.** A deposit isn't income. It's someone else's money that you're holding, and you'll likely owe most or all of it back.

**Keep it separate from operating funds.** At minimum a separate account, so you're never in a position where you've spent a deposit you now have to return. Some jurisdictions have specific requirements about how deposits must be held — check yours.

**Some places require notice** to the resident about where the deposit is held. Find out whether yours does.

**Track it per resident** — amount, date received, and which lease it's attached to. Obvious with one resident, easy to lose track of with four.

## What to document at move-in

This is the single highest-value twenty minutes in the whole process.

**Photograph everything.** Every wall, floor, fixture, appliance, and surface in their space and in shared areas. Timestamped. From angles you can replicate at move-out — this is the whole point.

**Walk through it together and write it down.** A simple condition checklist you both sign. Note existing scuffs, marks, and anything already damaged.

**Have them acknowledge the condition in writing.** A signed move-in inspection is worth more than any argument you could make later.

**For furnished spaces, inventory it.** What's in the room, condition of each item.

**Store all of it somewhere you'll find it in two years.** Not just on your phone.

The reason this matters: at move-out, a deduction is only as strong as your ability to show the condition changed. Without move-in documentation, you have your word against theirs, and that's a position you'll usually lose.

## What to put in the lease

- The deposit amount and when it's due
- What it covers
- Where it's held, if required
- The condition the space must be returned in
- How and when it will be returned
- That a signed move-in inspection is part of the agreement

Clear terms up front prevent most disagreements later, because you're both working from the same document rather than from memory.

## The house hack wrinkle

Two things are different when you live there.

**Shared spaces are harder to attribute.** Damage in a kitchen used by four people isn't obviously one person's. Document shared areas at every move-in and move-out, and be realistic about what you can fairly attribute.

**The relationship makes it awkward.** You'll be handling this with someone you've been living with. That's exactly why the documentation matters — it turns a judgment call into a comparison of two photographs.

## The one-line version

Collect a consistent, lawful amount. Keep it separate and don't spend it. Photograph everything at move-in and get it signed.

Do those three and the return conversation — which is the next post — is almost always easy.
