# What Makes a Good House Hack — And What Makes One Look Good But Fail

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

Once people understand house hacking, the next question is always the same: *okay, so what do I look for?*

It's a better question than it sounds, because the houses that make good house hacks are not the houses that show well. The listing photos that make you fall in love — open kitchen flowing into the great room, wide sightlines, airy single-story layout — describe a house that's genuinely hard to house hack.

Here's what actually matters.

## Separation beats square footage

If you take one thing from this: the best predictor of whether a house works is whether the people living in it can be apart from each other.

That means real doors, ideally a second entrance, somewhere other than the kitchen for a second person to exist, and bathrooms positioned so two people getting ready aren't negotiating.

A smaller house with good separation outperforms a bigger house where everyone funnels through the same space. It rents for more, turns over less, and you'll like living there more. Square footage only helps when it's divided in a way that gives people privacy.

This is why the layouts that sell best to families are often the worst candidates. Open-concept is designed so a parent can see the whole floor at once — the opposite of what you want here. Single-story homes put every bedroom on one hallway with no buffer.

**What works instead:** tri-levels, split-levels, anything with multiple zones. Houses where the geometry already separates people. In the Atlanta metro these are everywhere, and they're often priced below the open-concept homes everyone else is bidding on.

## The separate entrance changes everything

If a lower level or in-law space has its own exterior door, the whole calculation shifts.

A separate entrance means the space stops being "a room in your house" and starts being "somewhere someone lives." That changes who's willing to rent it, how long they stay, and what they'll pay.

Atlanta's terrain does you a favor here — sloped lots produce walk-out lower levels, which is why so much of our housing stock has this almost by accident. It's worth searching for specifically.

If a house has the space but not the door, price out adding one before you assume it's a dealbreaker. Sometimes it's straightforward. Find out before you write the offer.

## Bathrooms are the constraint nobody plans for

People count bedrooms. Almost nobody counts bathrooms until they're living with the consequences.

The ratio determines what you can charge and how much friction there is in the house. Four bedrooms sharing one bathroom is cheap to buy and hard to live in. The same four bedrooms with three bathrooms is a completely different property.

Comparing two houses at similar prices? The one with more bathrooms is usually the better house hack even with fewer bedrooms.

## Parking is worth more than it looks

This one surprises people. Parking rarely shows up in anyone's mental model of a good deal, and then it becomes the thing limiting how many people can comfortably live there.

If you're renting to multiple adults, most of them have cars. A two-car driveway with three adults is a problem every day. Look at the driveway, look at the street, count how many cars actually fit without anyone getting blocked in.

## Things that look like problems but aren't

**Dated finishes.** Cosmetics are cheap and they suppress the price. Good bones, good separation, ugly countertops is often the best deal on the board.

**Smaller than you wanted.** If the layout divides well, small is fine.

**Not the trendiest neighborhood.** Your residents care about commute, safety, and price.

## Things that look fine but aren't

**A basement that's "already finished."** Finished by whom, and how? A conversion done by someone who didn't know what they were doing can cost more than an unfinished space, because you're paying to undo work before you do work.

**A layout that "could easily be converted."** Sometimes true. Often it means moving plumbing, which is where budgets go to die. Get a real number from someone qualified.

**Shared-entry space priced like private space.** If the only way to reach the rentable area is through your living room, that's a spare-bedroom house hack — a completely valid model, but it earns spare-bedroom rents, not separate-unit rents. Just make sure you're underwriting it as what it is.

## The test I actually use

Walking a house, I'm asking one question the whole time: *can two people live here without constantly being in each other's way?*

Everything else is detail. If the answer is yes, run the numbers. If it's no, the price doesn't matter — because the version of your life where you live there is worse than the spreadsheet suggests.

The best house hack isn't the one with the highest projected income. It's the one you'll still be happy living in two years from now, that also happens to mostly pay for itself.
