# What You're Actually Responsible For as a Live-In Landlord

Most first-time house hackers have a vague sense that they owe their resident *something* and no clear picture of what. So they either over-deliver on things nobody expected or miss something basic.

Here's the practical shape of it. Specific requirements vary by state and city, so treat this as the framework and verify the details locally.

## What you owe them

**A habitable space.** The baseline: working plumbing, heat, electricity, hot water, a structurally sound and reasonably safe space. This isn't a courtesy — it's the floor, and it applies whether or not you live there too.

**Working safety equipment.** Smoke detectors, carbon monoxide detectors, functioning locks, safe exits. Test them, replace batteries, and document that you did.

**Timely repairs.** Urgent things urgently — no heat, no water, no working lock. Everything else within a reasonable window. Set an expectation and meet it: same day for urgent, a few days for the rest.

**Their privacy in their space.** Their room is theirs. Living in the same house doesn't create a right to enter it whenever you like. Give notice for anything non-emergency and keep to it. This is the one live-in landlords most often get casually wrong, and it's the fastest way to lose someone's trust.

**Notice before changes.** Anything affecting their living situation — work being done, a new resident moving in, changes to shared spaces — deserves advance notice.

**Their deposit, handled properly.** Held as required, returned within the required window, with any deduction itemized in writing and supported by photos. This is the most common source of bad feelings at the end of an otherwise fine tenancy, and it's entirely preventable by being organized and quick.

**Consistent, fair treatment.** Same rules, same standards, same responses across residents. If you have several, they will compare notes.

## What you don't owe them

**A social life.** You're not obligated to be friends, eat together, or hang out. Being pleasant is good practice; being their companion isn't part of the arrangement.

**Unlimited access to shared space.** You can define what's communal and what's primarily yours — you just have to do it *before* they move in, and make sure they have somewhere else to be.

**Solving their problems.** You're responsible for the property, not their finances, their relationships, or their choices.

**Silence about issues.** Being a decent landlord doesn't mean tolerating things that affect the house or your other residents.

**Below-market rent forever.** You can raise rent at renewal. Give real notice, explain it plainly, and be consistent — but it's your property.

## The area that's genuinely different when you live there

**Access and privacy cut both ways.** You have a right to your own space too. Define which parts of the house are yours, in writing, before anyone moves in.

**Shared-space maintenance is a joint reality.** You're responsible for the property. Everyone's responsible for not trashing it. Set expectations about cleaning, and be honest that yours count too — a landlord who enforces standards they don't follow has lost the room.

**Your own conduct is part of the deal.** Quiet hours apply to you. Your dishes count. Your guests count. You don't get an exemption because your name is on the deed.

## Things worth doing beyond the minimum

None of these are required. All of them make people stay.

- **Fix small things fast.** A house where things get fixed is a house people take care of.
- **Answer texts the same day**, even if it's just "got it, I'll look this weekend."
- **Give more notice than required** on anything that affects them.
- **Return deposits faster than you have to**, with an explanation.
- **Handle exits well.** People talk, and how you treat someone leaving shapes who's willing to move in.

Retention is the whole economics of this. A resident who renews saves you a turnover, a vacancy, and a screening cycle. Almost everything on that list costs less than one empty month.

## The frame

You owe them a safe, functional, private space and prompt attention when something's wrong. They owe you rent on time and reasonable care of the property.

Everything past that is what makes a house pleasant rather than merely functional — and it's the part that determines whether people stay two years or eleven months.
