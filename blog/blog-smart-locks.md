# Smart Locks: The Best Small Upgrade in a House Hack

Of all the cheap things you can do to a house hack, this one solves the most problems per dollar. Not because it's exciting — because it eliminates an entire category of recurring hassle.

## What it actually fixes

**No more key handoffs.** Meeting someone to give them a key, meeting them again to get it back, coordinating around schedules. All of it disappears. You send a code.

**No more rekeying between residents.** With traditional locks, you should change the lock every single turnover, because copies exist that you don't know about. With a code, you delete one and create another in ten seconds.

**No more "I got locked out."** The 11pm call where you drive across town, or the spare key hidden somewhere obvious. A code doesn't get left inside.

**A unique code per person.** Which means you know who came and went if you ever need to. Most models log entries.

**Temporary access, cleanly.** A cleaner, a contractor, a showing — a code that works on a schedule or expires. No key floating around afterward.

## Where to put them

**Every exterior entrance.** Front door, separate entrance to a lower level, side door.

**Every bedroom door.** This is the part people skip, and it matters. A lock on a bedroom door is a normal expectation in shared housing, not an insult — it's the baseline for someone feeling secure in a house with people they didn't choose.

Interior doors don't need the same hardware as exterior ones. Simple keypad handles are inexpensive and do the job.

**Storage areas** you want controlled — a shed, a garage, a locked closet.

## What to look for

**Keypad, at minimum.** WiFi connectivity is a nice-to-have that lets you manage codes remotely, but a good keypad lock with codes you set at the device solves most of it.

**Multiple user codes.** You want a unique one per resident, not a shared house code. Shared codes defeat the point.

**A physical key backup** or a battery jump option, for when batteries die. They will.

**Low-battery warning.** And replace batteries proactively at turnover rather than waiting.

**Auto-lock.** A door that locks itself is a door that's locked.

## The habits that make it work

**One code per person, never shared.** The moment everyone uses the same code, you've lost the tracking and you're back to rekeying.

**Delete codes the day someone moves out.** Put it on your turnover checklist so it's not something you remember to do.

**Change batteries at every turnover.** Cheap insurance against a lockout at a bad moment.

**Write the process into your lease.** That codes aren't to be shared, that unauthorized people don't get access, and that you'll provide a code on move-in.

**Keep a spare physical key somewhere secure off-site**, not hidden outside.

## What it costs versus what it saves

Exterior smart locks aren't expensive. Interior keypad handles are cheaper still.

Against that: rekeying a lock at every turnover, your time on key handoffs, the occasional locksmith call for a lockout, and the fact that you genuinely cannot verify that a traditional key wasn't copied.

For a house with a few bedrooms and a couple of entrances, this pays for itself within a turnover or two — and it removes friction from every single move-in and move-out after that.

## The privacy note

If you use a lock that logs entries, or you have a doorbell camera, **disclose it in the lease.** Not because it's a problem, but because surprising someone with it later is a trust issue you don't need.

The framing is easy: it's how access is managed, it's how you avoid rekeying, and it's part of what keeps the house secure for everyone.

## The one-line version

Codes instead of keys. One per person. Delete at move-out. Locks on bedroom doors too.

It's the cheapest thing on the list that makes both you and your residents safer, and it removes a recurring annoyance you'd otherwise deal with forever.
