# How to Be a Good Landlord in Your Own Home

Being a landlord is one job. Being a housemate is another. Doing both at once, in the same building, with the same person, is a third thing that nobody prepares you for.

Most people getting this wrong fail in one of two directions, and both are fixable.

## The two failure modes

**Too soft.** You're friendly, you're accommodating, you don't want to make it weird. Rent slides a few days. The dish situation goes unaddressed for three months. Someone's guest has been there two weeks and you haven't said anything. Eventually you're resentful, and the conversation you finally have is much bigger than it needed to be.

**Too rigid.** You over-correct into pure business. Every interaction is transactional, every issue is a documented notice. The house feels cold, nobody stays past the lease term, and you're doing a turnover every eleven months.

The version that works is neither. It's **rigorous about the systems and warm about the people.**

## Rigorous about systems

These are the things that never bend, for anyone:

- **The application process.** Same for everyone, including friends of friends.
- **The lease.** Written, signed, for every resident, even for one room.
- **The due date.** Same day, same platform, automated. Not "whenever you get a chance."
- **Documentation.** Photos at move-in, photos at move-out, written record of agreements.
- **Consistency between residents.** Same rules, same rent logic, same responses.

The reason these matter isn't strictness. It's that clear systems mean you're never making a personal judgment call. When the rule is written down and applies to everyone, enforcing it isn't about the person — you're both just following the thing you agreed to.

That's what makes it possible to be genuinely friendly with someone whose rent you collect.

## Warm about people

Everything else:

- **Answer your texts.** Same day for anything urgent.
- **Fix things fast**, including small things. It signals that this is a place where things get taken care of, and people treat the house accordingly.
- **Remember what they told you.** Their job, their sister's wedding, the interview they were nervous about. You live with this person.
- **Give notice on anything that affects them.** Work being done, guests you're having, a change to anything shared.
- **Be a good housemate yourself.** Your dishes count. Your noise counts. You don't get an exemption because your name is on the deed.

That last one matters more than people expect. A landlord who enforces quiet hours and then takes calls at midnight has lost the room.

## Where boundaries actually go

The hardest part is that you're not just a landlord and not just a housemate. Some practical lines that help:

**Define the shared space clearly, before anyone moves in.** Is the living room communal or primarily yours? Either answer is fine. Undefined is what causes problems — and if it's primarily yours, make sure residents have somewhere else to be.

**Don't manage their life.** You're responsible for the house, not their choices. Late nights, dating, dishes in their own room, how often they clean their bathroom — not your jurisdiction unless it affects shared space or the property.

**Don't over-share your own stress about the property.** Talking to a resident about how tight money is or how you're worried about the roof puts them in an awkward position. That's your business, not theirs.

**Keep money conversations in writing first, in person second.** Written so it's clear and unambiguous, then a conversation so it doesn't feel like a notice slipped under the door by someone you'll see at breakfast.

## Being fair when you have the power

You hold most of the leverage in this relationship. You own the building, you set the rules, and you're the one who decides on renewal. Most residents are aware of that even if nobody says it.

Being a good landlord means using that asymmetry carefully:

- Give more notice than the minimum on anything that affects them
- Return deposits promptly and explain any deduction with photos
- Don't use rent increases to push someone out — if it isn't working, say so directly
- Handle exits well. People talk, and how you treat someone leaving shapes who's willing to move in

## The test

A good live-in landlord is one where a resident would say two things: *the rules were clear from day one*, and *she actually fixed things.*

Clarity and responsiveness. Nearly everything else people worry about is downstream of getting those two right.

Do them consistently and you'll find residents stay longer, the house runs quieter, and the whole arrangement takes far less of your attention than you expected.
