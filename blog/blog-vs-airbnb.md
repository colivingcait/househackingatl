# House Hacking vs. Short-Term Rental

You have a spare space. You could rent it to someone who lives there, or you could rent it by the night. These produce genuinely different businesses.

> **Check your local rules before you plan a short-term rental.** Cities, counties, and HOAs regulate nightly stays and requirements change. Verify what applies to your specific address — I'm not covering that here and it's not optional.

## Income

**Short-term** generally produces more gross revenue per available night, sometimes substantially more.

**Long-term** produces less gross and much more predictable net.

The gap narrows considerably once you account for what short-term actually costs to run — cleaning between every stay, supplies, platform fees, higher utilities, furnishing and replacing, and vacancy on the nights nobody books. The headline nightly rate is not what you keep.

**Gross winner: short-term. Net winner: depends entirely on your occupancy and your cost structure.**

## Effort

This is the real difference and it's not close.

**Short-term** is a hospitality business. Turnovers every few days, guest messaging at all hours, reviews to manage, restocking, and constant coordination. It's a job, and if you hire it out, that's a meaningful share of the revenue.

**Long-term** is a rental. Screen once, sign a lease, collect rent, handle occasional repairs. A few hours a month.

**Mid-term** sits between: furnished like short-term, but a few turnovers a year instead of a few a week.

**Winner: long-term**, by a wide margin, if your time has value.

## Living with it

Worth thinking about seriously when the space is attached to your home.

**Short-term** means a rotating cast of strangers, most of whom you'll never meet, coming and going constantly. Some people are entirely fine with this. Others find it exhausting in a way they didn't anticipate.

**Long-term** means the same person for months or years. You know who's in your house.

**Winner: long-term**, for most people who live on the property.

## Volatility

**Short-term** income swings with season, events, local demand, platform algorithm changes, and competition. A strong month and a weak month can look very different.

**Long-term** is a lease. The number is the number.

For a house hack where you're relying on this income to make your housing affordable, predictability has real value.

**Winner: long-term.**

## Risk

**Short-term** carries regulatory risk that long-term generally doesn't — rules change, and a city that permits nightly stays today may not in three years. Building a plan around it means accepting that.

There's also more exposure to property damage and to insurance questions, since standard policies frequently exclude nightly rentals.

**Winner: long-term.**

## Where short-term genuinely wins

- A separate, fully self-contained unit with its own entrance, in a location with real visitor demand
- You actually enjoy hospitality — some people do, and they're good at it
- Your market has strong seasonal or event-driven demand
- You have capacity for the operational load, or budget to hire it

If those line up, the income can be meaningfully better.

## The option people skip

**Mid-term** — furnished, rented by the month rather than the night or the year.

Higher income than a standard lease. A few turnovers a year instead of constant. Residents who are out of the house most of the day. Predictable exits because assignments have end dates.

For a house hacker with a private furnished space, this is frequently the best of the three, and it's the one nobody considers because it doesn't have a famous platform attached to it.

## What I'd actually recommend for a first house hack

**Start long-term and unfurnished.** Simplest, lowest cost, least operational load, and your first year already has plenty going on.

Once you know how the house runs and whether you like this, you can furnish a space and test mid-term. That's a small, reversible step.

Short-term is the last one to consider, not the first — because it's the most work, the most regulated, and the most likely to change your relationship with your own home.
