# Top 3 Tips for Being a House Hacking Landlord

There's a long version of this, and it's worth reading eventually. But if you're about to have someone move into your house next month and you only have time for three things, these are the three.

They're not the most interesting advice. They're the advice that prevents the most problems.

---

## Tip 1 — Run the same process on everyone

Every applicant. Every time. No exceptions for people who seem nice, people who need a place urgently, or people who came recommended by someone you trust.

**The process:** written application, income verification against a standard you set in advance, background and rental history, a real conversation, a written lease.

**Why it's number one:** your house hack lives or dies on who lives in it. A good resident makes this feel like a lifestyle upgrade you're getting paid for. The wrong one makes you want to sell a perfectly good house.

And the mistake isn't usually bad judgment — it's *skipping the process entirely* because running one felt cold. Most of the time nothing bad happens, which is exactly why the habit sticks until the time it does.

**The corollary:** an empty room costs you one month. The wrong person costs you months of friction, your actual quality of life, and probably a turnover anyway. If you're not sure about someone, that's your answer.

---

## Tip 2 — Say the awkward things before they move in

Guests. Noise. Whether the living room is communal or primarily yours. Fridge shelves. Dishes. Parking. The thermostat.

Ten minutes of unglamorous conversation, before anyone signs.

**Why it works:** almost every conflict in a shared house traces back to an assumption nobody checked. Not a fight — an assumption. One person assumed overnight guests were fine. Another assumed the living room was shared. Neither was wrong. They just never asked.

**Bonus:** someone who reacts badly to being asked these questions has given you valuable information for free, before they moved in.

**Then write the answers down** — into the lease or a short house agreement. A conversation you both remember differently in six months isn't worth much.

---

## Tip 3 — Address small things immediately

The dish situation. The stuff accumulating in the living room. The guest who's been here nine days. The rent that came in three days late.

Say something small, early, without making it a whole thing.

**Why this is the hardest one:** you live here. You'll see this person tomorrow morning. Every instinct says to let it go and hope it resolves.

It doesn't resolve. It accumulates. And the conversation you have on day sixty isn't about dishes anymore — it's about two months of unspoken resentment, and it lands like an ambush.

*"Hey, can we figure out a dish system?"* on day four costs you nothing. That exact sentence on day sixty costs you the relationship.

**The reframe:** you're not being difficult. You're keeping the house easy to live in — for both of you. Residents in well-run houses stay longer, and "well-run" mostly means somebody addresses things while they're still small.

---

## The pattern underneath all three

Every one of these is about doing something slightly uncomfortable *early* to avoid something much more uncomfortable *later*.

Screening feels cold, until you've lived with someone you didn't screen. The expectations conversation feels awkward, until you've had the version of it that happens after a problem. Saying something on day four feels petty, until you've had the day-sixty version.

That's the whole job, really. Small discomfort now instead of large discomfort later.

Do these three consistently and you'll avoid most of what makes people say house hacking wasn't for them.
