# What a Good Lease Actually Covers

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

A lease for a room in a house you live in is a different document than a lease for a whole property. Most templates assume the owner isn't there, everyone has their own everything, and shared space isn't a category.

That gap is where the trouble lives. Here's what a shared-housing lease needs to address — so you can make sure yours does, whoever drafts it.

> **One note first:** leases are legal documents and the requirements vary by where you are. Have an attorney draft or review yours. This post is about what topics to make sure are covered, not about the language to use.

## Why the standard template isn't enough

A whole-property lease covers rent, term, deposit, and maintenance. All necessary, none sufficient.

What it doesn't cover is the entire category of *shared living* — kitchen, laundry, living room, parking, guests, noise. Those are exactly the things that cause friction in a house hack, and if they're not written down, they're an assumption. Assumptions are what conflicts are made of.

## The standard parts

Every lease covers these, and yours should be clear on them:

**Who, what, and when.** The parties, the specific space being rented, the term, and the start date.

**Rent.** Amount, due date, how it's paid, and what happens when it's late.

**Deposit.** Amount, and how and when it's returned.

**Utilities.** Which are included, which aren't, and if there's a cap or split, how it works.

**Maintenance.** Who's responsible for what, and how issues get reported.

**Ending it.** Notice required from each side, and what happens at the end of the term.

## The shared-housing parts

This is the section a standard template won't have, and the one that prevents most real problems.

**What space is exclusively theirs.** Which room, which bathroom if assigned, which parking spot, which storage.

**What space is shared, and what that means.** The kitchen, the living room, laundry. Whether the living room is genuinely communal or primarily yours. This one matters more than any other line in this section.

**Kitchen specifics.** Assigned fridge shelves, assigned cabinets, whether anything is communal.

**Laundry.** Availability, and whether there's a schedule.

**Guests.** How often, how long, overnight policy, notice expected.

**Quiet hours.** What time the house gets quiet.

**Cleaning.** Who's responsible for what, how often, and whether there's a cleaner and who pays.

**Smoking and pets.** Say it plainly.

**Parking.** Which spot, and where guests park.

None of these need to be elaborate. A sentence each is usually enough. The point isn't legalese — it's that when a question comes up, there's a written answer both of you already agreed to.

## What you're committing to

Leases usually describe what the resident owes. A good one for a shared house also says what you provide.

**Response time** for maintenance requests. Urgent versus non-urgent.

**How to reach you.** One channel.

**What's included** — utilities, internet, furnishings, supplies, cleaning. Specifically.

**Notice before you enter their space.** Even though you live there, their room is theirs. Saying so in writing sets the tone for everything else.

That last one does more for the relationship than people expect.

## Practical things worth having

**A move-in condition record.** Photos, attached or referenced. This is what makes deposit conversations simple instead of contentious.

**A rent review clause.** Something noting rent is reviewed annually. Turns a future increase from a confrontation into a scheduled event.

**An acknowledgment of house expectations.** If you have a separate one-page house agreement covering the shared-living items, referencing it in the lease connects the two.

## Use the same lease for everyone

This is the operational principle underneath all of it.

One document, same terms, every resident. When something comes up, you're not making a judgment call about a particular person — you're applying what everyone agreed to. That's what lets you be warm as a person while staying rigorous as an operator.

It also means every awkward situation you encounter becomes a clause you add for next time. Your lease gets better every year without you having to think about it.

## The thing a lease can't do

A lease doesn't prevent conflict. It resolves ambiguity.

The ten-minute conversation before someone moves in — going through guests, noise, the kitchen, the living room — is what actually prevents problems. The lease is where you write down what you agreed to, so that months later neither of you is relying on memory.

Have the conversation. Then put it in writing. Both, not one.
