# Turnover: What to Do Between Residents

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

Turnover is where most of the actual work in a house hack lives. It's also the most predictable — you usually know it's coming — which means it's the part most improvable by having a routine instead of improvising each time.

A good turnover takes days. A disorganized one takes weeks, and every one of those weeks is rent you didn't collect.

## Start before they leave

The mistake is treating turnover as something that begins when someone moves out. It begins when they tell you they're leaving.

**Confirm the date in writing** as soon as you know it.

**Ask to list the space while they're still there.** Most people are fine with it if you're respectful about scheduling. Showing an occupied room is slightly harder than showing an empty one, but filling the room the week they leave instead of a month later is worth the minor awkwardness.

**Walk the space with them, early.** Not the final walkthrough — an informal look a few weeks out. You'll spot what needs attention while there's still time to plan it, and you'll know whether anything is a real issue before it's a deposit conversation.

**Ask why they're leaving.** Genuinely. If it's a job or a move, that's information about your resident pool. If it's something about the house, that's information worth having.

## The move-out itself

**Do a documented walkthrough.** Photos of everything, same angles you used at move-in. This is why you took move-in photos.

**Handle the deposit promptly and clearly.** Whatever your process, do it on time and explain any deductions in writing with the photos attached. This is the single most common source of bad feelings at the end of an otherwise fine tenancy, and it's entirely avoidable by being organized and quick.

**Get the keys back and change the lock.** Every time, no exceptions. Copies exist that you don't know about.

## The reset

Have a standard list so you're not deciding each time.

**Deep clean.** Not a tidy — a real clean, including inside appliances, behind furniture, and the bathroom. This is worth paying for. It's the single highest-return line item in a turnover because it's what the next person sees first.

**Paint what needs it.** Usually touch-ups, sometimes a wall. Keeping the same color across all rooms makes this dramatically faster over time.

**Fix the small accumulated stuff.** The drawer that sticks, the loose towel bar, the light that flickers. Everything you noticed during the tenancy and didn't get to.

**Replace what's worn.** In a furnished space, this is where you assess the mattress, the seating, and anything that's had a few residents.

**Check the systems in that space.** Vents, drains, windows, smoke detector battery.

**Reset the small things.** Shower curtain, trash can, lightbulbs, drawer liners. Cheap items that make a space feel cared for rather than recycled.

## Photograph it every single time

Take fresh listing photos after every turnover, not once when you bought the house.

Photos age. Furniture moves, paint changes, the room looks different. A space that photographs well fills faster, and "well" mostly means: daylight, lights on, blinds open, nothing on the floor, shot from a corner.

This is fifteen minutes and it's the highest-leverage thing you do in the whole process.

## Reprice before you relist

Don't default to what the last person paid. Markets move in both directions.

Pull fresh comps — takes twenty minutes — and set the number based on what's happening now. Relisting at last year's rent is either leaving money on the table or pricing a room that sits empty for six weeks. Neither is a decision you meant to make.

## Build the standing list

The point of doing this deliberately once is that you never have to think it through again.

Write down your turnover checklist and your vendor list — cleaner, painter, handyman — and reuse them. After two or three turnovers you'll have a routine that runs in a few days without you making a single new decision.

That's what separates operators who find this manageable from ones who find it exhausting. It isn't tolerance. It's having done the thinking once.

## The number that actually matters

Track your days vacant, turnover to turnover.

That single number tells you whether your process is improving. It captures how fast you listed, how well you priced, how good your photos were, and how efficiently you reset the space — all in one figure you can compare over time.

Most people never measure it, which is why most people never get faster at it.
