# Shared Kitchens, Shared Laundry, Shared Everything: What Actually Works

*House Hacking Atlanta*

---

The bedrooms are the easy part. Everyone has a door, everyone has their own space, and nobody argues about a bedroom.

It's everything else — the kitchen, the laundry, the living room, the driveway — where shared houses either run smoothly or slowly grind on people. And most of what determines which one you get is decided before anyone moves in, by how the space is set up rather than by how considerate everyone is.

Here's what actually works.

## The kitchen is the whole ballgame

More friction happens in kitchens than everywhere else in a shared house combined. Three things fix most of it.

**Fridge space.** This is the number one source of low-grade tension, and it's almost entirely solvable with equipment. A second refrigerator changes a shared kitchen more than any other single change. If your kitchen has room and an outlet, add one — and if you're evaluating a property, check whether it does.

Barring that, assign shelves. Not as a rule people have to remember, but physically — labeled, obvious, permanent. Ambiguity is what causes the problem, not shortage.

**Counter and cabinet space.** Everyone needs somewhere their things live. Assigned cabinets work the same way assigned shelves do: the assignment prevents the conflict rather than resolving it.

**Enough of everything.** Two people wanting the same pan at 7pm is a scheduling problem you can buy your way out of. Extra pans are cheap. A second microwave is cheap. Anything that eliminates a daily negotiation is worth more than it costs.

## Laundry: access matters more than capacity

The question isn't whether there's a washer and dryer. It's whether someone can get to it without walking through anyone's private space or a room where someone might be sleeping.

If laundry is accessible from common space, it mostly takes care of itself. If it's through a bedroom, through a private area, or in a spot that requires crossing someone else's territory, it becomes a persistent low-grade issue that never quite gets resolved.

If access is awkward and can't be changed, a schedule helps — but a good location beats a good schedule every time.

## The living room needs an owner

This is the one people get wrong most often.

An undefined shared living room slowly becomes one person's living room. Somebody's things accumulate, somebody's habits set the tone, and nobody says anything for two months until it's a whole conversation.

Pick one:

**It's genuinely communal** — say so, and mean it. Everyone uses it, everyone's stuff can be there, and you're accepting that it'll look lived-in by several people.

**It's primarily yours** — say that too, up front, before anyone moves in. Then make sure residents have somewhere else to be. A house where the only place to exist outside your bedroom is a room you're not really welcome in is a house people leave.

Either is fine. Undefined is what causes problems.

## Bathrooms: assign them if you possibly can

If the ratio allows, assign bathrooms rather than sharing them. Even a bathroom shared by two people who each know it's "theirs and one other person's" runs better than one shared by four with no assignment.

If you can't assign, look at the morning. When does everyone leave? Two people with the same 7am schedule sharing one bathroom is a daily friction point that a small adjustment — a schedule, or a second mirror somewhere else — largely solves.

## Storage is invisible until it isn't

Every resident brings more belongings than their bedroom holds. If there's nowhere for the overflow, it ends up in shared space, where it becomes the living room problem above.

Give people somewhere: a labeled section of the garage, basement shelving, an assigned closet. It costs almost nothing and it prevents one of the most common slow-building sources of resentment in a shared house.

## Parking should be assigned, not negotiated

Assign spots. Write them down. Do it on day one.

Unassigned parking works fine until the day it doesn't, and then it's a conversation about something that felt too small to have a conversation about — which is exactly the kind that goes badly.

## The pattern underneath all of this

Every one of these fixes is the same fix: **remove the ambiguity before anyone has to navigate it.**

Assigned shelves, assigned bathrooms, assigned parking, a defined living room. None of these are about rules or strictness. They're about not making people negotiate the same small thing every week.

Houses where everyone has to be constantly considerate are exhausting. Houses where the setup already answered the question run themselves — and the residents in them stay much longer.

That's what you're actually buying with a second fridge.
